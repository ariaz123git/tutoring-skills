The one idea everything hangs off

Horizontal and vertical motion are independent — time is the only thing they share.
Horizontal: no force, so velocity is constant → x = vₓ·t  (aₓ = 0).
Vertical: gravity only, so it accelerates → use the kinematic equations with a_y = −9.8 m/s².
You solve the two axes separately and connect them through the shared time t.
1

Drop vs. shoot — the independence of x and y

aₓ = 0,  a_y = −g

Two balls leave a table at the same instant: one is simply dropped, the other is launched horizontally. Which lands first?

Ask first
  • Do the two balls hit the floor at the same time, or does the launched one stay up longer?
  • What is the vertical starting velocity of each ball? (Careful — the launched ball too.)
  • Does the horizontal velocity have any effect on how fast the ball falls?
Reveal the reasoning

They land together. Both balls start with zero vertical velocity and fall under the same gravity, so their vertical motion is identical. The launched ball's horizontal velocity moves it sideways but does nothing to its falling — horizontal and vertical are independent.

This is the whole subject in one picture: handle "how it falls" and "how it moves sideways" as two separate 1-D problems, joined only by the clock.

Watch it
The dashed line stays horizontal the whole way down — both balls are always at the same height.

Setting up — components & the two-column table

vₓ = v cosθ,  v_y = v sinθ

Before any projectile problem, split the launch velocity into its parts and make two columns — one for x, one for y. Pick "up = +" and write it down before you touch a number. (Sign slips are the single biggest point-loser here.)

The big 5 kinematic equations (constant acceleration)

  1. v = v0 + a t
  2. Δx = ½(v + v0) t
  3. Δx = v0 t + ½ a t2
  4. v2 = v02 + 2 a Δx
  5. Δx = v t − ½ a t2
You already know these — projectile motion just runs them per axis. On x, a = 0, so everything collapses to x = vₓ t. On y, a = −9.8, so the full set is in play. Each equation is "missing" one variable — pick the one that skips the quantity you don't have.
Ask first
  • For a launch speed v at angle θ above the horizontal, what are vₓ and v_y?
  • Which of the four kinematic equations apply on the x side, given aₓ = 0?
  • What is a_y, with sign, if up is positive?
Reveal the setup

Components: vₓ = v cosθ   v_y = v sinθ.

x-column (aₓ = 0): start from equation 5, Δx = v t − ½ aₓ t²; since aₓ = 0 the ½ aₓ t² term is zero, leaving x = vₓ t. Horizontal velocity never changes.

y-column (a_y = −9.8): the full kit — v_y = v_y0 + a_y t,  y = v_y0 t + ½ a_y t²,  v_y² = v_y0² + 2 a_y Δy.

The bridge: solve the y-column for the time t, then drop that same t into x = vₓ t.

Type 1 — horizontal launch from a height

v_y0 = 0
Example 1. A ball rolls off a 1.5 m table at 3.0 m/s. Find (a) the time to land, (b) how far from the table it lands, and (c) its velocity (speed and angle) at landing.
Ask first
  • What is the ball's vertical velocity the instant it leaves the table?
  • Which column — x or y — do you use to find the time? Why?
  • At landing, is the velocity horizontal, vertical, or a mix?
Reveal the solution
Step 1 — draw it & label what you know
The picture first. A 1.5 m table; the ball leaves flat at 3.0 m/s, so v_y0 = 0. Up = +: the drop is Δy = −1.5 m, and x along the ground is what we want.

(a) Time — use the y-column with v_y0 = 0, up = +, so the drop is Δy = −1.5 m:

Δy = ½ a_y t² → −1.5 = ½(−9.8)t² → t = √(2·1.5 / 9.8)

t ≈ 0.553 s

(b) Range — same time into the x-column:

x = vₓ t = 3.0 × 0.553 → x ≈ 1.66 m

(c) Landing velocity — recombine the components. vₓ = 3.0 (unchanged); v_y = a_y t = 9.8 × 0.553 ≈ 5.42 m/s downward:

v = √(3.0² + 5.42²) ≈ 6.20 m/s,   angle = tan⁻¹(5.42 / 3.0) ≈ 61° below horizontal

v ≈ 6.2 m/s, 61° below horizontal  [Forward 61° Downward]

Type 2 — angle launch on level ground

symmetry
Example 2. A ball is kicked at 20 m/s, 30° above the horizontal, on flat ground. Find (a) the maximum height, (b) the total time in the air, and (c) the range.
Ask first
  • What is the vertical velocity at the very top of the arc?
  • How does the time going up compare to the time coming down (same height)?
  • The same-height symmetry lets you find the flight time almost instantly here — why does a cliff break that?
Reveal the solution
Step 1 — draw it & label what you know
The picture first. Level ground; 20 m/s at 30° (the arc marks the angle). A symmetric arch — up to a peak of height Δy, then back to the same level a distance range away. Up = +.

Components: vₓ = 20 cos30 ≈ 17.32,  v_y0 = 20 sin30 = 10.0 m/s.

(a) Max height — at the top v_y = 0, so v_y² = v_y0² + 2a_y Δy → 0 = 10² + 2(−9.8)Δy:

Δy = 100 / 19.6 ≈ 5.10 m

(b) Total time — by symmetry, up-time = down-time, and v_y = 0 at the peak: t_up = v_y0/g = 10/9.8 ≈ 1.02 s → T = 2 t_up:

T ≈ 2.04 s

(c) Range — put the flight time into the x-column: R = vₓ T = 17.32 × 2.04:

R ≈ 35.3 m

6

Type 3 — launch from a cliff

quadratic in t

Connection — The method below is exactly the one-phase "launch from a height" trick from 1D kinematics: one quadratic in t covers the whole up-and-down flight. In 2D it's just the vertical column — the horizontal column shares the time, into the formula x = vₓ t.

Example 3. A ball is thrown at 15 m/s, 40° above the horizontal, from the top of a 25 m cliff. Find (a) the time in the air and (b) how far from the base of the cliff it lands.
Ask first
  • Taking up = +, what is the vertical displacement Δy when the ball lands at the base? (Sign!)
  • Why can't you use the symmetric "time up = time down" shortcut directly here?
  • Once you have the flight time, is finding the horizontal distance any different from Type 1 or 2?
Reveal the solution — the quadratic
Step 1 — draw it & label what you know
The picture first. A 25 m cliff; 15 m/s at 40°. It lands below the launch, so there is no up/down symmetry — the vertical column needs a quadratic. Up = +.

Components: vₓ = 15 cos40 ≈ 11.49,  v_y0 = 15 sin40 ≈ 9.64 m/s.

(a) Time — up = +, lands 25 m below the launch, so Δy = −25:

Δy = v_y0 t + ½a_y t² → −25 = 9.64 t − 4.9 t²

Rearrange to 4.9 t² − 9.64 t − 25 = 0 and use the quadratic formula:

t = [9.64 + √(9.64² + 4·4.9·25)] / (2·4.9) = [9.64 + √583] / 9.8

t ≈ 3.45 s  (take the positive root; the negative one is unphysical).

(b) Distance — same t into the x-column: x = vₓ t = 11.49 × 3.45 → x ≈ 39.6 m

Five traps to avoid

The wrong ideaThe fix
"Horizontal and vertical motion affect each other."They're independent — only time is shared.
"A projectile flies straight until it runs out of push, then drops."Gravity acts the whole time; there's never a horizontal force.
"At the top of the arc the velocity is zero."Only v_y = 0 there; vₓ keeps going unchanged.
"Heavier objects fall faster."The trajectory doesn't depend on mass.
"The level-ground time shortcut works even off a cliff."No — a height difference breaks the up/down symmetry. Solve the y-column with a quadratic, then x = vₓ t.
